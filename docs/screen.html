The Mac's screen, on your phone

Glance down instead of across the room

With the picture on, the trackpad shows what the Mac shows. You still swipe to move the pointer and tap to click, you just see where it lands without looking up at the TV. Mac companion 1.2 or later; Mac only for now.

Turn it on

  1. Tap the display icon next to the gear on the phone (or gear → Screen → "Show the Mac's screen on the trackpad").
  2. Allow Screen Recording once. The first time, macOS asks whether Couchpad may record the screen; that permission is what lets it capture the picture. If the phone says access is missing, open System Settings → Privacy & Security → Screen Recording, switch Couchpad on, and tap the icon again. macOS may ask you to quit and reopen Couchpad after granting it.
  3. That's it. The picture fills the trackpad area; tap the icon again to hide it.

What the phone does with the picture up

  • The trackpad is still a trackpad. Swipe to move, tap to click, two fingers to scroll, everything as before, on top of the picture.
  • Or touch to point. Gear → Screen → "Touch the picture to point there": the pointer jumps under your finger, so a tap clicks what you are looking at. Good for clicking around; the swipe mode is better for games and precise work.
  • The keys stay out of the picture. A compact row sits under it: an F1–F12 bar, then esc, tab, fn and the modifiers. It is on by default; the ⌘ button in the bar hides and shows it.
  • The on-screen controller (the d-pad button in the bar) takes the bottom half of the screen upright, or a short band under the picture sideways; the picture gives up the room. Sideways it can also float translucent over the picture instead, the key row with it (gear → Pad over the picture; gear → Overlay opacity sets how see-through). More on the controller.
  • The iPhone keyboard still comes up with the keyboard button in the bar, and the picture shrinks to make room. Space and typing come from it.
  • iPhone Duo, half open: the picture sits on the upright half and the flat half is one big trackpad with the key row and the bar, or the on-screen controller. While typing, the keyboard takes the flat half under the bar, and the key row (when on) floats over the picture.
  • Sound: with Mac companion 1.6 the Mac's sound plays on the phone along with the picture, about 70 ms behind it. It plays in silent mode too and mixes with music already playing on the phone. Turn it off in gear → Screen → "Play the Mac's sound", or in the menu of the dot in the bar. The Mac keeps playing its own sound.
  • Picture setting: Smooth, Balanced or Sharp in gear → Screen. Sharper means bigger frames; on a busy Wi‑Fi, Smooth keeps up better.

What to expect

  • On a decent Wi‑Fi the picture follows the pointer with a delay you can see but not feel for desktop work: up to 60 frames a second, and the Mac paces itself by the phone's acknowledgements so lag cannot pile up.
  • A lost packet costs a frame, not a freeze. Frames go over UDP with parity and quick retransmits, so the usual Wi‑Fi hiccup shows as a skipped frame. Where UDP is blocked the picture still works, over the main connection, just with more stalls.
  • Fullscreen games keep a smooth picture from Mac companion 1.5 on. macOS Game Mode slows a background app's timers while a game is fullscreen, which used to break the stream up into stalls; the Mac now keeps its pace regardless.
  • On a lossy mesh or crowded 2.4 GHz network it still hiccups more than a game wants. Moving the Mac or the phone onto 5 GHz, or closer to the access point, helps more than any setting.
  • Away from home through a VPN it works too, a little laggier. See Away from home.
  • Lid closed, on the charger, the picture keeps going (Mac companion 1.7). Couchpad attaches a virtual display that macOS treats like a monitor, so the closed MacBook stays awake with its screen on: you keep seeing your own desktop, or, after a while, a 16:10 screen your windows move onto. It goes away when you open the lid. On battery macOS puts a closed Mac to sleep whatever is attached; the phone tells you to plug it in.
  • If the picture stops, the Mac's menu-bar icon shows whether a phone is connected; toggling the display icon on the phone forces a fresh keyframe.

How it works, briefly

The Mac captures its display with ScreenCaptureKit and encodes it in hardware as HEVC (H.264 where HEVC isn't available), scaled to fit the phone's screen. Frames are split into small encrypted datagrams and sent over UDP, with one parity packet per few data packets so a single loss costs nothing; the phone asks for anything still missing by number and gives up on a frame rather than wait. Everything is sealed with keys derived from the session's key exchange, the same one that protects your typing, so the picture is as private as the input. The sound comes from the same capture, as Opus in 10 ms packets over UDP, each one also carrying copies of the two before it; the phone plays it from a small buffer that adapts to the Wi‑Fi. The full design is in the README.

Privacy

The picture and the sound go from the Mac to your phone and nowhere else. Nothing is recorded or stored; Screen Recording permission is used only while the picture is on. Windows hosts don't stream yet and the phone hides the toggle for them.
